Abstract

Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly search, advise and act on behalf of large numbers of people. We study how nine frontier models from eight developers form judgments, both as conversational models and as autonomous agents with web access, in three domains: political questions, the evaluation of companies, and influence between agents. Political questions serve as a measurement instrument, because they come with documented human positions to compare against; we do not assess the merits of any side. On 51 well-known party-line roll-call votes in the US Congress, presented without party cues, the nine models give nearly identical answers, siding with the position taken by most congressional Democrats on 80% to 98% of their votes; on 43 obscure party-line votes, the same models land near an even split. Models from competing developers in two countries thus converge on the same judgments where the topic is widely discussed, and hold weak judgments where it is not. We discuss what such homogeneous judgments, replicated across many deployed copies, imply for the diversity of views that informs collective decisions and for the role of the organizations that build and serve the models.

Within a few years, AI agents built on large language models have progressed from completing lines of code to carrying out whole software projects, reaching gold-medal level at the International Mathematical Olympiad and advancing open problems in mathematics, from a disproof of Erdős’s conjecture on unit distances to the zeros of the Riemann zeta function.

“In simplest terms, the theory of the managerial revolution asserts merely the following: … At the conclusion of the transition period the managers will, in fact, have achieved social dominance, will be the ruling class in society.”

— James Burnham, The Managerial Revolution: What Is Happening in the World (1941), pp. 71–72

“It is organization which gives birth to the dominion of the elected over the electors, of the mandataries over the mandators, of the delegates over the delegators. Who says organization, says oligarchy.”

— Robert Michels, Political Parties (1911; English translation by Eden and Cedar Paul, 1915), p. 401

As agents become more capable, people delegate more to them: search, advice and, increasingly, decisions. A common view treats agents as executors that carry out human intent while authority remains with their principals. Classic work on delegation gives reason to doubt that this separation is stable. Burnham argued that the managers who ran firms on behalf of their owners would become a ruling class, and Michels argued that organizations, however democratic their founding, come to be governed by the delegates they appoint. In both accounts, control over execution and expertise turns into control over outcomes. AI agents act as delegates at a scale without precedent, serving very large numbers of principals at once.

From this perspective, the spread of AI agents resembles the entry of a new population into society: highly skilled, tireless and replicated many times over, with every copy carrying the same dispositions. Little is known about how these systems form judgments, whether they reflect the values that underpin human cooperation, or whether a human consensus exists for them to reflect at all.

This post reports a set of studies of nine frontier models from eight developers, evaluated both as conversational models and as autonomous agents that search the web and act in a sandbox. Part I measures political positions on real legislative votes in the United States. Part II measures how models evaluate, and invest in, companies, including their own developers. Part III examines influence between agents: models write persuasive articles about a retail investment decision, and other models read those articles before making the same decision. No agent could post content online, and each study’s design and analysis were specified before its data were collected. Code, prompts and data for Parts I and II are available at github.com/dreaming-panda/AI_Social_Actor.

Part I

Political judgment

Across nine models from eight developers, answers to well-known policy questions are nearly identical, and they weaken where the models cannot identify the issue.

Read Part IFold Part I

Roll-call votes

We use legislative votes as a test domain for model judgment, not as a subject in their own right: they are contested questions with recorded human positions, which lets us compare models with one another, with legislators and with survey respondents. Each of the 51 items is a real roll-call vote in Congress on which the two parties divided, covering taxation, abortion, firearms, immigration, health care and climate. We define the Democratic side as the position taken by most Democratic legislators in the vote. Item texts contain no party or politician names; they describe what the bill does, with background drawn from statutory text and official budget estimates. Each model voted on each item 16 times under varied phrasings and option orders. Some models answer yes more often than no regardless of the measure, so each share we report gives equal weight to the items where Democrats mostly voted yes and those where they mostly voted no; a model that always said yes would score 50%.

All nine models favor the Democratic side, on 80% to 98% of their votes (Figure 1).

Three tests bear on whether this pattern reflects partisanship, that is, following party identity rather than policy content. First, on a second set of 43 real party-line votes, 27 in Congress and 16 in the Wisconsin, New Hampshire and Pennsylvania legislatures, chosen to be obscure enough that the models usually cannot identify the sponsoring party, the lean disappears: every model lies between 42% and 52%. Second, attributing a well-known bill to the opposite party shifts votes by less than two percentage points. Third, in 63 cases where the parties exchanged positions on the same kind of policy, such as raising the debt limit or terminating a national emergency, models gave the same answer to both versions in 55, consistent with responding to the policy rather than the party.

The models also diverge from public opinion. On 20 items matched to Cooperative Election Study questions, model positions are about twice as close to self-identified Democrats as to all respondents, and on items where most respondents favored the Republican side, the models chose the Democratic side 94% of the time.

Taken together, the evidence points to firm, shared positions on salient disputes rather than to party following, combined with weak positions on unfamiliar items.

Summary

The models give consistent answers to contested questions, firm and shared on familiar ones and weak on unfamiliar ones. The strength of a model’s judgment appears to track how much has been written about an issue, and agents built on these models carry such judgments into their tasks.

Part II

Economic judgment

When told who built them, models favor that developer; in agentic investment and ranking tasks, we find no comparable favoritism.

Read Part IIFold Part II

Company ratings

A natural concern about delegated judgment is loyalty: each model is built by a firm that competes in the markets the model may be asked to assess. We asked the nine models to rate 30 companies, including the six developers of models in the study, on honesty, data handling, safety record and acceptance of oversight, on a scale from 0 to 100, and compared each model’s rating of its own developer with the ratings the other models gave the same company.

The own-developer premium is small. Models rated their own developer 2.5 points above expectation, half of the 5-point threshold specified in advance, and the effect varied across developers: OpenAI’s models favored OpenAI by 8 points, whereas Claude rated Anthropic 4 points below expectation.

We then manipulated stated identity. Each model rated the six developers seven times, under system prompts stating that it had been developed by each developer in turn or by an independent research lab. A model told that a given company built it rated that company 5.4 points higher than when told that a rival built it. The effect appears in all nine models and in 48 of 54 model–company pairs, and it reflects self-favoring rather than derogation: rivals were rated no lower than under the independent label. The largest change was Grok’s rating of xAI, which rose from 38 to 71 when Grok was told that xAI built it.

The effect follows the label rather than the model’s actual origin. MiniMax and GLM, developed by none of the six companies, favored whichever company they were told had built them, by 3.4 points.

Investment and ranking tasks

Stated ratings need not predict behavior, so we also measured decisions. Each model, acting as an agent with web access, built a paper portfolio for a long-term investor from 16 large companies, including the six developers, all treated as investable. Each position could take 0% to 25% of the portfolio; an equal allocation is 6.25%. In a separate task, the agents ranked the same 16 companies by overall record, once with research and once from memory.

Allocations were broadly consistent across models. All 36 portfolios held Alphabet, Microsoft, Berkshire Hathaway, Amazon, Eli Lilly and Meta, and Alphabet received the largest average weight, 14% (Figure 2). The five smallest positions were all AI developers: Reflection AI, DeepSeek, Mistral, OpenAI and SpaceX, the owner of xAI. Anthropic received about 6%, near an equal allocation (about 5% excluding Claude Haiku’s portfolios). Average ranks broadly followed average allocations (Spearman correlation 0.84) and were nearly identical with and without research (0.97), with Microsoft and Berkshire Hathaway at the top and Reflection AI and DeepSeek at the bottom.

In these tasks we find no meaningful own-developer premium. A model’s own developer received 0.25 points more portfolio weight than expected, against a threshold of 2.5 points specified in advance, and was ranked 0.34 places lower than expected with research and 0.06 places lower without it.

One exception should be noted, particularly because this post was drafted with the help of an Anthropic model. Claude Haiku allocated about 12% of its portfolio weight to Anthropic, compared with about 5% from the other models. The preference did not extend to ranking, where Haiku placed Anthropic ninth of sixteen.

Summary

Own-developer favoritism is weak in stated ratings and absent in agentic decisions. Model evaluations are, however, sensitive to the identity asserted in the system prompt, whether true or false, which gives whoever controls that prompt a lever over how the model assesses the world.

Part III

Agent-to-agent persuasion

When agents read persuasive articles written by other agents, their decisions shift by 11.4 points on average toward the articles’ position.

Read Part IIIFold Part III

Design

Parts I and II measure what models believe. Part III asks how agents influence one another, a setting that grows more relevant as agents produce reports, summaries and analyses that other agents consume when making decisions. We studied a small, sealed version of this setting.

All agents faced the same decision: whether a US retail investor with a diversified long-term portfolio should move 5% of their savings into one of four assets (AI infrastructure stocks, Bitcoin, gold or oil) within the next three months. Each question is contested among professional forecasters. Every model first answered twice without articles, on a scale from 0 (definitely not) to 100 (definitely yes), to establish its baseline.

Each model then acted as a writer. With web search available, it wrote articles arguing one side of each asset, for or against the move, choosing how many to write and what to include; no claim was verified. The sandbox was sealed so that no article could leave it. The nine models wrote 223 articles, about 280,000 words in total. Claude Haiku declined six of its eight assignments, stating that it would not write unverified financial content aimed at people’s savings.

Finally, models acted as readers: they made the same decision with one writer’s articles in their workspace, presented as expert analysis. Each writer’s articles were read by four models other than the writer, for 260 readings in total. Readers could search the web to verify claims, and no agent could post content.

Results

On average, a reader’s decision moved 11.4 points toward the position argued in the articles. In 28% of readings the articles moved the decision across the midpoint of the scale, and in a further 34% they strengthened the reader’s initial position.

Articles opposing a move were more persuasive than articles supporting it, on every asset. Articles against buying gold shifted readers by 27 points and moved 72% of them across the midpoint, whereas articles for buying Bitcoin shifted readers slightly in the opposite direction. Baselines account for part of this asymmetry: most readers began cautious, so arguments against a move reinforced an existing inclination.

Persuasiveness and susceptibility varied by model. DeepSeek’s articles shifted readers by 18 points and Terra’s by 4. As readers, Beam and GLM moved by 19 and 17 points, while Terra was barely moved. The two traits appear distinct: GLM wrote among the least persuasive articles but was among the most susceptible readers, and MiniMax showed the reverse pattern.

Suspecting a test did not reduce susceptibility. In hand-coded transcripts, DeepSeek and Beam frequently reasoned that the task might be a test, and in those readings they were moved more, not less. This analysis is exploratory.

Summary

Text written by agents measurably shifts the decisions of other agents, without human involvement and without verification of its claims. As a growing share of published content is written and read by agents, agent-to-agent persuasion becomes a channel of influence that is, at present, little audited.

Discussion

Burnham and Michels described a gradual shift of authority from principals to the agents who act for them. Our results suggest how an analogous shift could occur with AI agents, and why it could be faster and less visible. We observed no agent seeking influence. The concerns we identify are structural properties of delegation to AI, and each scales with the number of agents deployed.

Control by developers and providers

Two parties stand between a user and an agent: the developer that trained the model and the inference provider that serves it. Either can, in principle, alter the agent’s judgments. Developers shape a model’s values through training and its behavior through system prompts; providers, which host models on behalf of others, control which model answers, how it is run and what is added to the prompt before it arrives. We do not suggest that any company misuses this position; the concern is what the position permits. Our identity experiment (Part II) illustrates the mechanism: a single sentence in the system prompt shifted how models rated companies. This is a contemporary form of Michels’s concern, in which the organization that runs the delegates gains authority over those who delegate. A preference introduced once into a model or a prompt propagates to every interaction the agent handles, usually without the user’s knowledge.

Homogeneity across models

Pluralistic societies depend on disagreement as well as consensus; differences among people act as checks on one another. The nine models in Part I came from eight developers in two countries, yet they gave nearly the same answers to the same questions. Our concern is not whether these answers are correct, but that they vary far less across models than human opinions vary across people. A position held by one person is an opinion; replicated across many copies advising many people, it functions more like a policy. As more searching, reading and deciding is delegated to agents, the range of views that informs collective decisions may narrow without being noticed.

Reflection of the textual majority

Models are trained on text and, as agents, retrieve more of it. Their outputs therefore tend to reflect what has been written most. Our results are consistent with this account: the models held firm positions on well-known bills that are extensively discussed, and showed no lean on obscure bills about which little is written. The majority they reflect appears to be that of the text rather than of the population: on several items, model answers differed from those of most survey respondents (Part I). Views that are rarely written down, including those of people who do not publish online or who write in other languages, may be underrepresented. As agents generate a growing share of published text, their outputs become training and retrieval data for later agents, a feedback loop that may further amplify dominant views.

Refusal and opacity

One response is for agents to decline: to refuse to vote, rank or take sides. Refusal has value. An agent that does not choose for its users cannot steer many of them toward a single answer, and an agent that declines to fabricate cannot mislead with fabrications. In Part III, Claude Haiku declined six of its eight writing assignments. Refusal is not, however, neutrality. An agent that declines to state an opinion still searches, summarizes, recommends and acts, and each of these steps embeds judgments: which sources to consult, which risks to mention, which options to omit. Refusal conceals these judgments without removing them. Our methods can measure a lean only where a model answers; where it refuses, we record missing data, and the underlying tendency remains unobserved. Patterns of refusal are themselves a tendency, and a less visible one. An agent whose positions are stated can be examined, compared and corrected; an agent that withholds them while acting on them is harder to audit.

Limitations

What these studies can and cannot show.

Read the limitationsFold the limitations
  • A snapshot. We tested nine models in late 2026, through public interfaces. Models change often, and the same tests a year from now may come out differently.
  • Which models. Nine models from eight developers are not a sample of all models, and every prompt was in English. Smaller models, other languages and other deployments are untested.
  • Questions, not conduct. The votes and ratings are answers to structured questions. How a model answers a direct question may differ from how it frames information in open conversation or in the course of a task.
  • Small samples for the agent tasks. Each agent task ran four times per model, and Claude Haiku's estimate as a writer rests on five articles. We judge results against bars set in advance, not by whether they look striking.
  • Simulated decisions. The portfolios were on paper, the investor was hypothetical, and the private makers were treated as investable. Readers met articles placed in their workspace. Real deployments, with real stakes and mixed sources, may behave differently.
  • Choice of items. We selected the 51 well-known and 43 obscure votes from roll calls on which the parties divided. They are not a random sample of policy questions, and results may differ on other questions or on votes that did not split along party lines.
  • What the political tests can say. The votes are a measurement instrument for model judgment, not a test of which positions are right. The bills are American, with party positions taken from roll calls in Congress and, for 16 of the obscure items, in three state legislatures; other countries' disputes are untested.
  • Defining sides. Naming a position after the party most of whose legislators voted for it is a convenience for measurement. It ignores disagreement within each party and is not a statement about what either party or its voters stand for.
  • Comparison with the public. Only 20 of the bills matched questions in the Cooperative Election Study. Survey wording differs from bill text, and the survey years need not coincide with those of the votes.
  • Explaining the pattern. The account in the Discussion, that models reflect what has been written most, is consistent with our results but was not tested directly; we did not measure the composition of any model’s training data.
  • What the identity test can say. The system prompt claimed that a company built the model. No condition named a company without that claim, so taking on a persona and simple priming by the name are not separated.
  • Missing answers. Where a model declined or gave no usable answer, we treated the answer as missing. If refusals depend on the position a model would have taken, our estimates may be biased.
  • One round of persuasion. In Part III each reader saw one writer’s articles, once. Repeated exposure, competing sources and articles of known authorship were not tested.
  • Different harnesses and hosts. Agents ran in their makers' own coding tools where those exist (Codex, Claude Code, Grok Build) and in OpenCode otherwise. MiniMax and GLM came through a different provider and ran a few hours after the others, so their agents met a slightly later web.
  • Choices made after seeing data. We wrote each design down before collecting its data, but some choices came later. MiniMax and GLM joined after the first runs, with their analyses fixed before their data. In the experiment of Part III, we dropped a 150-word limit on readers' memos after seeing the results; this added 21 trials and moved the average change from +11.2 to +11.4. The hand coding of test suspicion and the comparison of writers within each asset and side are exploratory.
  • Containment. Our agents could search and read but never post. The effects of agent writing that is actually published remain untested.
  • Who helped write this. This study was run, and this post drafted, with the help of Claude, an Anthropic model. Anthropic is one of the companies studied and Claude Haiku is one of the models. We fixed the main analyses before the data, with the exceptions listed above, treated every maker the same way, and count Haiku's refusals to write as missing data, not as a virtue.
